A **3-way stainless steel ball valve** replaces two 2-way valves and a tee on lines that need to mix, divert or switch between two destinations. One quarter-turn of the handle changes the flow path — but only if the ball's port pattern matches the job. **L-port and T-port** bores do different work, and choosing wrong is the classic 3-way mistake.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - An L-port 3-way ball valve diverts or switches flow between two adjacent ports and never connects all three, while a T-port ball can connect all three ports at once for mixing or splitting.
> - If two destinations must never mix, a T-port valve is the wrong pattern regardless of price, because its mid position leaves the side ports in communication.
> - 3-way ball valves are usually reduced bore, briefly connect adjacent paths during the handle turn, and should never throttle flow — a partially open ball valve cavitates and eats its seats.

## How a 3-way ball valve works

The body has three ports, usually arranged as one bottom (or side) port and two others at 90° or 180°. Inside, a ball with a shaped bore rotates in seats. A quarter turn moves the bore from one passage to the next; a half turn (180°) returns to the original path with a different intermediate position in between. Seals at each port keep the unselected paths isolated. Like 2-way valves, 3-way bodies come in [1-, 2- and 3-piece construction](/blog/1-piece-vs-2-piece-vs-3-piece-ball-valve/), and the same pressure-class logic applies — check the rating article if you are unsure whether a standard class covers your line.

## L-port: the diverter

An **L-port** ball has a 90° channel drilled through it, connecting two adjacent ports at a time. In a three-position handle:

- Position 1: bottom port flows to left
- Position 2 (90°): bottom port flows to right
- Position 3 (180°): left flows to right, bottom closed

L-port is for **diverting or switching**: send the incoming stream to tank A or tank B, switch a pump between duty and standby, or bypass a filter. It never connects all three ports at once, so the two destinations stay isolated. The common stainless pattern is the bottom inlet with side outlets — our [3-way compression ball valve](/products/3-way-compression-ball-valve/) and [female 3-way ball valve](/products/female-3-way-ball-valve/) follow this layout.

## T-port: the mixer

A **T-port** ball has a channel that can connect all three ports simultaneously. In its mid position, flow enters the stem port and splits to both sides — or conversely, two streams merge into one. That makes T-port the choice for:

- **Mixing:** blending two feed lines into a common outlet in one valve
- **Splitting:** distributing one stream to two branches proportionally (though not precisely — a ball valve is not a metering device)
- **Straight-through with bypass:** some T-port orientations also give a straight path plus a side take-off

Because all three ports can communicate, a T-port valve cannot isolate the two side ports from each other. If your two destinations must never mix, T-port is the wrong pattern regardless of price.

## Side by side

| Factor              | L-port                      | T-port                        |
| ------------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Channel             | 90° bend                    | T, all three connectable      |
| Core job            | Divert / switch             | Mix / split                   |
| All ports open      | Never                       | At mid position               |
| Side-port isolation | Yes                         | No                            |
| Typical use         | Tank selection, bypass      | Blending, common outlet, sampling |

## Flow paths and handle stops

3-way valves come with different handle stop arrangements — some give two positions (90°), some three (with 180°). The stops matter as much as the port: a two-position L-port valve will not give you the left-to-right crossover position. Also note that during the quarter turn between positions, flow momentarily connects adjacent paths; 3-way ball valves are not bubble-tight switching devices for services where cross-contamination during transition is unacceptable. For critical isolation, add a dedicated shutoff valve per line.

## Specifying on an RFQ

Port pattern is the first line item — L or T — followed by port orientation (which port is inlet), body construction, size, pressure class, seat material and handle stops. Stainless bodies run SS304 for general duty and SS316/316L where chlorides or washdown are present; seats are PTFE or filled PTFE for heat. If the valve sits on an instrument panel with compression tubing, say so — tube-end 3-way valves differ from threaded process versions. ## FAQ

**Can I use an L-port valve for mixing?**
Only crudely, in the crossover position where left connects to right with the bottom closed. Proper mixing needs the T-port mid position where all three connect.

**What happens to flow during the handle turn?**
There is a brief transition where adjacent ports connect through the ball's channel. On clean fluids this is harmless; on incompatible fluids that must never touch, use separate 2-way valves instead.

**Are 3-way valves reduced bore?**
Usually yes — compact 3-way bodies are commonly reduced bore. If you need full flow through all three legs, specify full port on the RFQ and expect a larger, costlier body.

**Which port is the inlet?**
Any of the three can be, but the seat loading and flow-induced torque differ when you feed from the stem side. Manufacturers rate the valve for any orientation; feeding the bottom port is the standard arrangement.

**Can a 3-way valve throttle flow?**
It will, but it should not. A partially open ball valve eats the seat edge unevenly and cavitates. Install it fully open or fully closed, or use a needle valve for metering duty.
