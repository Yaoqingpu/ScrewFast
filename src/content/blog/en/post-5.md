Correct installation matters as much as the alloy. Most first-time leaks on **stainless steel compression fittings** come from short insertion, hard tube or skipping the ferrule order — not from the forging itself. The good news: five disciplined steps prevent nearly all of them.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - A compression joint seals only when the tube is cut square, deburred, fully seated to the body shoulder and tightened in the prescribed ferrule order — most first-time leaks trace to one of those four, not to the fitting.
> - The standard makeup is finger-tight plus 1-1/4 to 1-1/2 turns for common stainless double-ferrule sizes — more damages the ferrules, less leaves the joint under-made.
> - Ferrules deform permanently on first makeup: every remake needs new ferrules on a freshly cut tube section, and mixing ferrule brands in one fitting is a leak waiting to happen.

## Tools and preparation

You need less than you think: a proper **tube cutter** (not a hacksaw — saws leave an angled, work-hardened end), a deburring tool for the ID and OD, and calipers if the tube origin is uncertain. Cut on a marked line, rotate the cutter rather than forcing it, and check the end against a square. A ragged or oval end cannot seat to the shoulder and cannot take an even bite — the leak is decided at this step, long before the nut turns.

## The five steps, in order

1. **Cut square and deburr.** ID and OD burrs both matter: an ID burr restricts flow and starts a turbulence point; an OD burr sits between the ferrule and tube and prevents the bite.
2. **Slide on nut, then back ferrule, then front ferrule — in that order.** On a double-ferrule fitting the front (conical) ferrule faces the body; reversing them produces a joint that assembles but never seals.
3. **Insert the tube to the body shoulder.** Full insertion positions the ferrules over the correct tube zone. Partial insertion — the most common field error — leaves the bite on the wrong section and the joint weeps under pressure.
4. **Tighten to the factory prescription.** The standard makeup for common stainless double-ferrule sizes is finger-tight plus 1-1/4 to 1-1/2 turns, or the maker's gauge where one exists. Over-tightening swages the ferrules past their design set and damages them; under-tightening leaves the bite incomplete.
5. **Pressure-test before boxing the panel.** Leak-detection fluid at working pressure finds what ears cannot — test every new joint, not just the suspicious ones.

## Tube hardness

Use tubing that meets the hardness range for double-ferrule service — for most stainless instrument work, fully annealed tube in the fitting maker's specified range. Hard hydraulic tube can refuse a proper bite: the ferrule skates instead of swaging, and the joint leaks on gas service no matter how carefully you tighten. If the tube's history is unknown, say so on the RFQ — tube hardness changes the recommended ferrule set and sometimes the whole fitting choice.

## Leak troubleshooting

When a new joint weeps, diagnose before you reach for the wrench:

| Symptom                          | Likely cause                          | Fix                                  |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Weeps immediately at pressure    | Under-tightened or tube not bottomed  | Tighten in small increments; re-seat |
| Leaks after days of service      | Hard tube — ferrule never bit         | Replace tube with annealed; new ferrules |
| Leaks at remake                  | Reused ferrules on old bite marks     | New ferrules; cut tube to fresh section |
| Leak plus visible scoring        | Mixed ferrule brands or debris in set | Rebuild with one matched brand set   |
| Leaks though "fully tight"       | Over-tightened — ferrule crushed      | Replace ferrules; remake correctly   |

The pattern to respect: one corrective turn, then test. Grinding the nut around and around looking for tightness only destroys parts.

## Remaking a used joint

Open the nut, withdraw the tube, and discard the ferrules — they are single-use items that deformed permanently on first makeup. Inspect the tube: if the bite marks are deep or the section is work-hardened, cut back to an undamaged length and remake from step one with new ferrules. The fitting body and nut survive many cycles; the ferrules never do.

## FAQ

**How many turns past finger-tight should I go?**
For common stainless double-ferrule sizes, 1-1/4 to 1-1/2 turns past finger-tight is the standard prescription, or the maker's gap gauge reading where one exists. Large sizes and some series differ — follow the size-specific spec when it exists.

**Can I reuse compression ferrules?**
No. Ferrules deform permanently on first makeup and hold their set. Reused ferrules sit on old bite marks, seal poorly and usually leak immediately on gas service. New ferrules cost little; remaking with them is the only correct procedure.

**Why does my joint leak even though I tightened hard?**
Either it is under-made (tube never bottomed, so the bite landed in the wrong place) or over-made (the ferrule crushed past its sealing geometry). Hard tube can also prevent any bite at all. Diagnose with the table above rather than adding more torque.

**Do I need a special tool to install compression fittings?**
No special tooling — a tube cutter, deburring tool and wrenches cover the job. Preset torque tools and make-up gauges help on production panels where every joint must match, but hand assembly to the turn-count prescription is the field standard.

**What tube hardness works for double-ferrule fittings?**
Fully annealed stainless tubing in the hardness range the fitting maker specifies seats most reliably. Hard hydraulic tube resists the swage and leaks on gas lines — flag tube hardness on the RFQ if there is any doubt.
