A **ferrule** is the small metal ring that makes a compression fitting seal — the component that actually bites into the tube and locks the joint. Buyers searching "ferrule fitting" usually mean the whole compression joint, but understanding the ring itself explains why these joints hold pressure, why they sometimes leak, and why the remake rules are non-negotiable.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - A ferrule is a single-use metal ring that the fitting nut swages into the tube surface: the bite displaces tube metal to create a pressure-tight seal and mechanical grip in one deformation.
> - In a double-ferrule set, the front ferrule forms the primary seal against the body while the back ferrule grips the tube against pull-out and absorbs vibration.
> - A ferrule must be harder than the tube it bites, and ferrules deform permanently on first makeup — replace them at every remake and never mix brands in one fitting.

## What the ferrule actually does

When the nut tightens, the ferrule is driven into the body cone, which squeezes it onto the tube surface. Two things happen in that single stroke: the ferrule's leading edge **bites** — displacing a controlled ridge of tube metal — while its face is pressed into a matching cone in the body to form the **seal**. The displaced tube metal locks into the ferrule's groove, so the joint resists both pressure and pull-out mechanically. No sealant is involved; the seal is cold metal deformed against cold metal, which is why the joint survives vibration and remaking better than anything relying on tape or compound.

## Front ferrule vs back ferrule

In a double-ferrule set the two rings divide the labor:

| Aspect             | Front ferrule                                                        | Back ferrule                                                                       |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Role               | Conical ring facing the body                                          | Ring behind it, driven forward by the front                                         |
| What it forms / grips | The primary metal-to-metal seal against the body seat; takes the first share of the sealing load | Swages harder onto the tube; grips against pull-out and flexes to absorb vibration so the primary seal is not worked loose |
| Failure if worn    | The joint loses its primary seal                                      | Grip against pull-out is lost and vibration reaches the seal, working it loose      |

A single-ferrule design asks one ring to do both jobs and does it well enough on stationary lines. The split design is what makes the joint forgiving on vibrating instrument panels — the back ferrule takes the abuse so the front ferrule keeps sealing. How the complete joint goes together around these rings is covered in [what is a compression fitting](/blog/what-is-a-compression-fitting/), and the pattern-level choice in [single ferrule vs double ferrule](/blog/single-ferrule-vs-double-ferrule/).

## The hardness rule

A ferrule must be harder than the tube it bites, or it cannot swage and lock — it skates on the surface and the joint leaks no matter how tight the nut goes. This is why fully annealed stainless tube is the default for instrument work: soft enough to accept the bite, strong enough to hold pressure. Hard hydraulic tube is the classic failure — the ferrule never gets its grip and the joint weeps on gas service. If tube hardness is unknown, say so on the RFQ; it changes the recommended ferrule set.

## Ferrule materials and matching to service

Ferrule material follows the tube and the media. SS304 ferrules serve general duty on SS304 tube; SS316/316L pairs with SS316 systems in chlorides and marine exposure; brass ferrules belong on copper tube, where a hard stainless ring would score or crack the soft surface; and exotic service — seawater at temperature, strong acids — pushes ferrules to alloys such as Monel or Hastelloy. Mismatched hardness in either direction fails: too soft and the ring deforms without biting, too hard and it damages the tube instead of sealing.

## Remake and replacement rules

Two rules prevent most ferrule-related callbacks. First, **ferrules are single-use**: they deform permanently on first makeup, and a remade joint with old ferrules seals on damaged geometry. Replace the rings every time the joint opens, and cut the tube back to an undamaged section before reassembly. Second, **never mix ferrule brands in one fitting** — front ferrule, back ferrule, nut and body are matched as a set by geometry and hardness, and mixed sets produce leaks at best and pulled joints at worst. If the brand of the existing fitting is unknown, replace the whole set rather than gambling on a partial match.

Use ferrule fittings on analyzer, gauge and pneumatic tube runs where welding is unwanted. ## FAQ

**Is a ferrule the same as an olive?**
Yes — "olive" is the British trade name for the same compression ring. Catalogs may say ferrule, olive or compression ring; the part and its single-use remake rule are identical.

**Why do ferrules come in pairs on some fittings?**
Double-ferrule sets split sealing (front ring) from gripping and vibration absorption (back ring). Single-ferrule fittings combine both jobs in one ring. The pair is what makes double-ferrule joints tolerant of vibration and remaking on instrument panels.

**Can I reuse a ferrule if it looks undamaged?**
No. The working deformation is microscopic: the ring took its permanent set on first makeup even if it looks fine. Reused ferrules seat on old geometry and leak — especially on gas service. New ferrules at every remake is the rule.

**What happens if the ferrule is softer than the tube?**
It deforms instead of biting, never locks into the tube surface, and the joint pulls out or leaks under pressure. A harder ferrule set — or annealed tube — fixes it; identify which on the RFQ.

**Are ferrules interchangeable between brands?**
No. Each maker matches ring geometry, hardness and nut drive to its own body cone. Mixed-brand sets feel assembleable but seal on line contact instead of the designed band, and the joint fails early. Replace the complete set from one brand.
